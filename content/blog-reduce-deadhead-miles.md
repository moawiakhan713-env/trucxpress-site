Every mile you run empty costs you fuel, hours of service and wear on the truck, and pays nothing. On our dispatch desk, cutting deadhead is the cheapest raise we can give a carrier.

## The real cost of an empty mile

Add up fuel, maintenance, insurance and the truck payment, and most owner-operators spend well over a dollar per mile just to move. Run 400 deadhead miles a week and you burn hundreds of dollars and a full day of driving time with nothing to show for it. Over a year that is tens of thousands of dollars gone quietly.

## Why deadhead happens

Deadhead is usually a planning problem, not a freight problem. Booking loads one at a time, grabbing the best rate on the board without asking what is on the other end, strands trucks in weak markets where the only way out is empty.

## Three strategies that work

- **Trip pairing and return-load planning.** Book the outbound and the reload together, so a strong headhaul never dumps you into a dead zone.
- **Lane preference matching.** Decide the regions that work for your business and filter freight to fit, not the other way around.
- **Appointment timing.** Tight pickup and delivery windows prevent the missed-reload spiral where one late dock turns into two empty days.

## Measure the right number

Do not judge a load only by its rate per loaded mile. Divide the pay by **all** the miles, including the empty miles to get there and the empty miles to the next pickup. A $3.00 load with 200 deadhead miles can pay less than a $2.60 load that starts right where you unload.

> Our rule on the desk: plan the reload before you accept the load.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much deadhead is normal?
Many carriers run 10% to 20% empty miles. Above that, the truck is likely being booked one load at a time without a plan for the reload.

### Is it ever worth deadheading on purpose?
Yes. A short empty move from a weak market into a strong one can earn more over the week than waiting for a cheap load. That is a planned reposition, not wasted miles.

### Does running a set lane reduce deadhead?
Often. Regular lanes with known reloads cut empty miles, though they can limit rates when the spot market is strong.

### Does a dispatcher really reduce deadhead?
A good one does, because they plan the reload before you deliver instead of after. That planning is most of the difference.

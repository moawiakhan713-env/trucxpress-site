Reefer Loads: What Pays and What to Avoid

Reefer loads often pay more than dry van, but the rate on the board is only half the story. On our desk we judge reefer loads by the rate minus the time and risk they carry. Here is what we look for.

Loads that usually pay well

  • Seasonal produce in peak season, when growers need trucks fast
  • Frozen loads with firm appointments and quick loading
  • Consistent food distribution lanes with reliable shippers and fast docks
  • Time-sensitive loads that need a dependable carrier more than the cheapest one

Hidden costs that eat the rate

  • Long waits at produce sheds. Loading can take hours, especially in peak season. A high rate with a six-hour wait may pay less per hour than a slower lane with a quick load.
  • Lumper fees. Many receivers require paid unloading help. Confirm who pays and how you get reimbursed, and always keep the receipt.
  • Reefer fuel. The unit burns fuel the whole time, including while you wait.
  • Washouts. Some food shippers require a clean trailer and a washout receipt.

Protect yourself from temperature claims

Temperature claims are the biggest risk in reefer freight. Protect yourself every load:

  1. Get the set point and continuous or cycle mode written on the rate confirmation.
  2. Pre-cool the trailer before you back into the door.
  3. Check and record pulp temperatures for produce, and note them on the bill of lading if they are out of range.
  4. Keep the unit's download or temperature records until the load is paid.
  5. Report any problem immediately rather than at delivery.

Seasons that move reefer rates

Produce moves north with the seasons. Winter volume concentrates in the Southwest, Florida and Texas, including the Nogales and Yuma areas near our Phoenix office. Spring brings Southeast and California volume, and summer pushes north and toward the Northwest. Holiday food demand lifts frozen and chilled volume in the fall.

Building a reefer week that pays

  1. Start with your best-known shippers and lanes, where loading times are predictable.
  2. Use the season: position near producing regions when they peak.
  3. Fill gaps with dry loads instead of running empty.
  4. Avoid booking a long produce load right before your required home time, since sheds often run late.

When to say no

A high rate is not worth it when the pickup has a reputation for all-day waits with no detention, when the set point is unclear, or when the broker will not confirm temperature details in writing.

Frequently asked questions

Are reefer rates always higher than dry van?

Usually, but not always. In slow produce periods, reefer carriers often take dry freight to stay moving, which narrows the gap.

Can I haul dry freight in a reefer trailer?

Yes. Many reefer carriers haul dry loads between reefer loads to cut empty miles.

What insurance do brokers want for reefer?

Many ask for reefer breakdown coverage as part of cargo insurance, on top of standard liability and cargo limits.

What is pulp temperature?

It is the temperature inside the product itself, measured with a probe, not the air temperature in the trailer. Recording it at pickup protects you if the product was loaded warm.
